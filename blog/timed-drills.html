Timed drills made me faster at everything

Speed is not the enemy of understanding. When the basics become automatic, your brain is free for the hard part.

Devon K., Grade 9  ·  January 22  ·  5 min read

I used to think timed math drills were the enemy of "real" learning. Understanding is what matters, I told myself, not speed — and besides, the clock made me anxious. I was half right, which is the most dangerous way to be wrong.

The thing I missed is that speed and understanding are not opposites. They are stages. You understand something first, slowly and carefully. Then, if you practice it enough, it becomes automatic, and automatic is where the real power is. The point of a timed drill is not to rush your thinking. It is to make the small stuff so effortless that you barely have to think about it at all.

Working memory is tiny

Your working memory — the mental scratchpad you use to hold ideas while you work — is shockingly small. It can juggle only a few things at once. If solving one step of an algebra problem forces you to stop and slowly compute seven times eight, that multiplication is eating a slot on your scratchpad. You have fewer slots left for the actual algebra, which is the hard and interesting part.

Automate the basics and you free up your mind for the thinking that actually matters.

When the times tables are automatic, seven times eight is not a calculation. It is a fact you simply have, the way you have your own name. That freed slot goes toward the real problem. This is why fast and slow students often understand a concept equally well, but the fast one gets further into the problem before running out of mental room.

What changed for me

I started doing short, timed arithmetic drills — sixty seconds at a time, a few rounds a day. At first my scores were embarrassing and the timer made my stomach tight. But the drills were short enough that the anxiety never had time to build, and the daily repetition did exactly what repetition does. Within a month, facts that used to require a pause came out instantly.

The surprise was where it showed up. Not just in arithmetic, but in geometry, in chemistry, in physics — anywhere I had to do quick calculation in the middle of a bigger idea. I was not smarter. I had just stopped spending my limited attention on the cheap stuff, so there was more of it left for the expensive stuff.

A note about the clock

If timers make you nervous, start with the goal of beating only yourself. Ignore everyone else. Write down your score each day and try to nudge it up a little. The clock stops being a threat when it is measuring your own progress instead of comparing you to a stranger. Speed, it turns out, is just understanding that has been practiced until it is quiet.
