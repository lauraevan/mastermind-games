Learning Spanish vocabulary that actually sticks

Flashcards alone never worked for me. Spaced repetition plus real sentences finally made the words stay.

Sofia L., Grade 7  ·  December 15  ·  5 min read

I have made and abandoned more Spanish flashcard decks than I can count. I would write out fifty words, drill them for an evening, feel great, and then discover a week later that I remembered maybe six. The words never seemed to move from the card into my actual head. For a while I decided I just was not a "languages person," which is a comfortable lie that lets you stop trying.

It turned out I was doing two specific things wrong, and fixing them changed everything. The first was that I reviewed words in a block, all at once, the same day I learned them. The second was that I learned them alone — a Spanish word on one side, an English word on the other, floating with no context. Both mistakes felt efficient. Both were quietly sabotaging me.

Space the reviews out

The fix for the first mistake is called spaced repetition, and it is one of the most reliably proven ideas in the science of memory. Instead of reviewing a word ten times today, you review it today, then in two days, then in five, then in two weeks. Each time you almost forget it, then just barely retrieve it. That little struggle to remember is precisely what carves the word into long-term memory. Reviewing something you already remember perfectly teaches you almost nothing.

Study a word just as you are about to forget it. The struggle to recall is what makes it stick.

This felt wrong at first, because letting yourself forget a little seems like the opposite of studying. But that mild forgetting is not the enemy; it is the mechanism. A word you retrieve with effort on day five is far more permanent than a word you glanced at five times on day one.

Never learn a word alone

The second fix was to stop learning bare words and start learning them inside sentences. "Perro" on a flashcard is an abstract sound attached to a translation. "El perro corre en el parque" — the dog runs in the park — is a little scene. It gives the word a home: a gender, a role, other words it likes to stand next to. When I needed "perro" later, I did not reach for an isolated card. I heard the whole sentence, and the word came with it.

Context also teaches you things a two-sided card never can. It shows you that adjectives usually follow the noun, that the article changes with gender, that verbs shift depending on who is doing the action. You absorb the grammar quietly, as a side effect of learning the vocabulary, instead of studying it as a separate miserable subject.

My routine now

These days I add only a handful of new words at a time, always inside a sentence I can picture. I let a spaced schedule decide when to bring each one back, so I am always reviewing the words on the edge of forgetting rather than the ones I already own. And I try to actually use the new words the same week, even in a clumsy sentence to myself, because using a word is the strongest review there is.

It is slower per day than my old cram-fifty-cards evenings. It is enormously faster per word that actually stays. I am not a "languages person." I just finally started working with how memory actually behaves instead of against it.
