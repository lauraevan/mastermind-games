How I finally understood fractions

Fractions felt like a foreign language until a teacher told me to stop memorizing rules and start drawing pictures.

Marcus T., Grade 6  ·  February 19  ·  5 min read

I want to be honest about how bad it was. For a long time, fractions were the part of math where I simply gave up and guessed. I knew the words — numerator, denominator, common, improper — but they were just sounds. I could recite the rule "flip the second fraction and multiply" without having any idea why anyone would flip anything.

The problem, I eventually realized, was that I was trying to memorize a pile of disconnected rules. Add these but not those. Flip this one but not that one. Find a common something. Each rule was a separate thing to forget under pressure, and I forgot them constantly.

The pizza that fixed everything

My teacher, Mr. Owens, watched me fail a quiz and did not give me another rule. He gave me a marker and told me to draw. A fraction, he said, is just a picture of a whole thing cut into equal pieces. The bottom number tells you how many pieces the whole is cut into. The top number tells you how many of those pieces you have. That is the entire idea. Everything else follows from it.

Do not memorize the rule. Draw the picture, and the rule will explain itself.

Suddenly one-half plus one-fourth was not a rule to recall; it was a picture I could see. Half a pizza is two of the four quarters. Two quarters plus one quarter is three quarters. I did not need to be told to find a common denominator. The picture forced it: you cannot add pieces of different sizes, so you cut them into the same size first. The rule was just describing the drawing.

Why we cut into equal pieces

The word "equal" turned out to be the one I had been ignoring. Thirds are only thirds if all three pieces are the same size. That is why you cannot just add the tops and bottoms of two fractions — the pieces are different sizes, so the count is meaningless until you make them match. Once I understood that, comparing fractions stopped being a mystery too. Which is bigger, three-fifths or two-fifths? If the pieces are the same size, more pieces is more. Easy.

Even the strange rules made sense once I drew them. Dividing by a fraction means asking how many of that piece fit inside your amount. How many quarters fit in a whole pizza? Four. That is why dividing by one-quarter is the same as multiplying by four. The "flip and multiply" trick was not a random spell. It was a shortcut for a question I could finally picture.

What I would tell my past self

If fractions are hard for you, I do not think you are bad at math. I think you have been handed rules without pictures, which is like being handed the answers to a puzzle you have never seen. Slow down. Draw the bar, cut it into equal pieces, shade what you have. It feels babyish and slow at first. It is neither. It is the fastest way I know to make the rules stop being magic and start being obvious.

I still draw pictures on my scratch paper during tests. My friends think it is funny. My grades do not.
